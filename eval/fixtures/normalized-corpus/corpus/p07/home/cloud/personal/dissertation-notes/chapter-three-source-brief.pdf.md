# Chapter three source brief

Arrangement, annotation, and evidence in the Keller-Roth papers

**Chapter claim.** The third chapter follows the material life of a judgment after it leaves a letter: copied into a reading slip, altered by translation, and later condensed into an argument. The archive is not treated as a transparent storehouse. Its folders, interruptions, and annotations shape what can responsibly be said.

**Source set.** The core set combines selected Keller-Roth letters with reading lists, annotated copies, and a small group of workshop notes. The aim is to compare the forms of evidence, not to turn every trace into a single continuous story.

|  Document type | Chapter use  |
| --- | --- |
|  **Letter** | Establishes a dated exchange and its immediate language of response.  |
|  **Reading slip** | Preserves a title, page cue, or translation decision at working scale.  |
|  **Annotated copy** | Shows how a text was marked without assuming why every mark was made.  |
|  **Workshop note** | Records the later social setting in which a phrase was discussed.  |

**Method.** Each passage should be introduced with its material situation: what the document is, what can be read directly, and what remains an inference. This keeps the chapter's interpretation proportional to the evidence.

## Evidence sequence

**1. Establish the document.** Record the visible date, the local folder context, and the language of the passage. Where a scan crops a margin or hides the reverse side, say so rather than silently filling the gap.

**2. Describe the reuse.** A phrase may recur as a quotation, a translation, an objection, or a shorthand. The chapter should name the form of reuse before making a larger claim about continuity.

**3. Compare without flattening.** The later typescripts often smooth the hesitations present in the correspondence. Preserve that contrast. It is part of the history of how an argument became publishable.

**Question at review** Evidence needed

**Was the phrase read or merely remembered?** A page cue, a marginal note, or a dated discussion in the letters.

**Did translation alter the claim?** The source term, the working gloss, and the surrounding sentence.

**Does the folder order matter?** Visible sequence markers or a note from the donor context.

**Can the passage enter the chapter?** A legible source, a stable reference, and a stated level of certainty.

**Writing principle.** The prose should make room for a negative result. A connection that cannot be established is still informative when the archive shows the limits of the available record.

### Before the next draft

- Confirm every quoted phrase against a legible image or a verified transcription.
- Keep a private source log for uncertain readings; do not turn that log into a polished bibliography.
- Ask the archive liaison only for questions that cannot be resolved from the available surrogates.
- Review multilingual passages with the translation workshop before fixing an English gloss in the chapter.

**Desired result.** The chapter should leave the reader with a clear sense of documentary texture: a research argument built from correspondence, annotations, and careful absences rather than from an artificially complete citation trail.
